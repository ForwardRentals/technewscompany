The Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday authorized SpaceX to launch up to 1 million satellites to run artificial intelligence workloads in orbit, according to Gizmodo. The approval covers a constellation called Starmind and is the agency's first for a full network of orbital data centers.

## What was approved

SpaceX filed its application on January 30. It describes Starmind as a way of "capturing solar energy in space to power low-cost, high-performance AI compute for Earth." The satellites would be solar-powered and linked to one another by high-bandwidth laser connections so they function as interconnected data centers.

For scale, SpaceX currently operates just over 11,000 Starlink satellites, so Starmind would raise the number of satellites in Earth orbit roughly 50-fold. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the action "opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure" needed for U.S. leadership.

## Deployment conditions

The authorization sets milestones rather than a launch date:

- First operational satellite: by November 2033
- 10% of the constellation (100,000 satellites): by November 2035
- 50% of the constellation: by November 2038
- Full constellation: by 2040

## Limits and objections

The FCC did not grant one request. SpaceX had asked to move satellites operating above 600 km (372 miles) into graveyard orbits at the end of their lives rather than deorbit them. The commission said the company had not provided enough information about its disposal process.

The Secure World Foundation had urged regulators to weigh cumulative risks to orbital sustainability, and the American Astronomical Society warned the constellation could significantly interfere with ground-based astronomy. According to Gizmodo, the FCC declined to carry out a comprehensive environmental assessment, saying several of the concerns fell outside its jurisdiction.

## Why it matters

Orbital computing is an early-stage idea that other companies, including Google and Blue Origin, are also pursuing. The approval does not guarantee the network will be built. Deploying hundreds of thousands of satellites would depend heavily on SpaceX's Starship rocket, because the launch cadence and payload capacity needed exceed those of conventional vehicles. Whether the economics and engineering, including heat dissipation, can work at that scale remains unresolved.
